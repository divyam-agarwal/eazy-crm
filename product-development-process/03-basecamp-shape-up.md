# 03 — Basecamp: Shape Up

**Source confidence: high.** *Shape Up* is published free and in full by Basecamp, written by
**Ryan Singer**, who developed the method there. Every claim below is from the book.

Shape Up is the most complete published alternative to sprint-based development, and its core
inversion is this: **fix the time, vary the scope — and make that a commitment, not an aspiration.**

---

## The cycle

- **Six weeks of building**, then **two weeks of cool-down.**
- Six weeks is chosen deliberately: long enough to finish something meaningful, short enough that
  you can see the end from the beginning — which is what actually forces scope discipline.
- **Cool-down** is unstructured: bug fixes, exploration, cleanup, and the shaping and betting for
  the next cycle.

## Shaping

Before anything is bet on, someone **shapes** the work. Shaped work is:

- **Not a spec** — deliberately under-specified, leaving real design decisions to the team building it.
- **Not a wireframe** — deliberately abstract, so it doesn't over-constrain.
- Bounded by an **appetite**: not "how long will this take?" but **"how much time is this worth?"**
  Appetite is an input constraint, not an estimate.

A **pitch** — the written artifact — contains:

1. **Problem** — the raw idea or the specific pain, with the motivation.
2. **Appetite** — how much time we're willing to spend (typically six weeks, or two).
3. **Solution** — the shaped design, at the right level of abstraction.
4. **Rabbit holes** — specific technical or design traps, called out and pre-solved so the team
   doesn't fall into them.
5. **No-gos** — explicit non-goals; things deliberately excluded to make the appetite achievable.

Sections 4 and 5 are the ones most teams omit and the ones that make the method work. **Rabbit
holes and no-gos are how you make a fixed time budget realistic** before anyone has committed.

## The betting table

The decision meeting. Concretely, from the book:

- **When:** during the **two-week cool-down** between cycles.
- **Who:** at Basecamp, **the CEO (the last word on product), the CTO, a senior programmer, and a
  product strategist.** Deliberately small, and deliberately senior enough that **there is no
  further approval step afterwards.**
- **How long:** typically **one to two hours**.
- **Preparation:** participants read the pitches beforehand; informal conversation over the preceding
  weeks establishes context. The meeting itself is purely deliberation and decision.
- **Input:** pitches shaped during the previous six weeks, plus the occasional older pitch someone
  has chosen to revive.

### "No backlog" — what it actually means

Basecamp does not maintain a prioritised queue of future work. They keep only **"a few good options
to consider."** Rejected pitches are **not systematically archived**; if an idea genuinely matters,
someone will advocate for it again and it will resurface on its own.

The reasoning: a backlog is a growing pile of obligations that nobody has re-examined, and
maintaining it consumes real time while creating an illusion of commitment. **Letting ideas die and
requiring them to be re-argued is a filter, not a loss.** Anything important survives; anything that
doesn't survive wasn't important.

### What a bet commits to

A bet is a two-way commitment, and both directions are enforced:

1. **Uninterrupted time.** The team gets the **entire** six weeks for the bet. No side quests, no
   "quick favours," no interrupts. The book is explicit that even small interruptions destroy the
   momentum the fixed cycle depends on.
2. **The circuit breaker.** *"Teams have to ship the work within the amount of time that we bet. If
   they don't finish, by default the project doesn't get an extension."* The project is dropped, not
   extended.
3. **Six weeks plus recovery** — the cool-down is part of the deal, not slack to be reclaimed.

**The circuit breaker is the load-bearing rule.** Because there's no extension, the team must cut
scope to fit — which is exactly the behaviour you want and exactly what a deadline-with-extensions
fails to produce. It also caps the cost of a bad bet at six weeks, which is why "betting" is a more
honest word than "planning."

## Why the vocabulary is deliberate

The book insists on "betting" rather than "planning" because it sets different expectations:

- **A bet has a payout.** Work is shaped into a six-week box specifically so something meaningful
  is finished at the end. Not "progress" — a finished thing.
- **A bet can be lost**, and that's an acceptable, bounded outcome.
- **You don't owe a bet anything after it resolves.** No sunk-cost obligation to keep funding it.

## What to steal

1. **Appetite instead of estimate.** "This is worth two weeks" is a decision you can make and defend.
   "This will take two weeks" is a prediction you'll be wrong about. Appetite converts an unanswerable
   question into a business judgement.
2. **Write rabbit holes and no-gos into every pitch.** These two sections do more for on-time delivery
   than any estimation technique.
3. **The circuit breaker.** Even in a small team: if it isn't done by the deadline, it stops and gets
   re-argued rather than automatically extended. This is uncomfortable exactly once.
4. **Kill the backlog, or at least stop grooming it.** If nobody will re-pitch it, it wasn't going to
   get built anyway.
5. **Protect the cycle.** Uninterrupted time is the actual scarce resource. If your team is
   interruptible, none of the rest of this works.
6. **Small, senior, final decision group.** A one-to-two-hour meeting whose output is binding beats
   a chain of approvals every time.
