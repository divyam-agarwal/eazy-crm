# 04 — Atlassian: DACI

**Source confidence: high.** The Atlassian Team Playbook is published by Atlassian as a product, and
DACI is one of its "plays" with a documented step-by-step format.

DACI is narrower than the other entries here — it isn't a whole development process, it's a
**decision-making framework**. It's included because it addresses the failure mode that quietly
wrecks the most projects: **a decision that nobody actually owns, made by a group, revisited forever.**

---

## The four roles

| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| **Driver** | *"The person responsible for corralling stakeholders, collating all the necessary information, determining the scope of the decision, and getting a decision made by the agreed date."* Owns the **process**, not the outcome |
| **Approver** | **Exactly one person** with final decision-making authority. Singular by design — this converts approval from a passive rubber-stamp into active leadership |
| **Contributors** | Subject-matter experts who provide recommendations and insight. **They have input but no vote** |
| **Informed** | People affected by the outcome who need to be told once it's made, but who don't participate in the deliberation |

The two design decisions that matter:

1. **Driver ≠ Approver.** Separating "who makes this happen" from "who decides" is what stops
   decisions from stalling. The Driver has authority over the *process* — including a deadline —
   without needing authority over the *outcome*.
2. **Contributors have input but not a vote.** This is stated explicitly, and it's the honest part.
   Most stalled decisions are groups pretending to seek consensus while nobody can actually close.
   Naming the Approver up front means everyone knows what kind of conversation they're in.

---

## Running the play

The published play is a **60-minute meeting with 15 minutes of prep**:

| Step | Time | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 min | Set up a shared collaboration document |
| 2 | 5 min | Choose the **Driver** |
| 3 | 5 min | Identify the **Approver** and the **Contributors** |
| 4 | 5 min | List who needs to be **Informed** afterwards |
| 5 | 10 min | Fill in the template: decision details, background, supporting data, key factors, options |
| 6 | 10 min | Gather input and explore alternatives |
| 7 | 10 min | Document action items and their owners |
| 8 | 10 min | Communicate the final decision and the reasoning behind it |

Note that **steps 1–4 (20 of the 60 minutes) are spent purely on establishing who is doing what.**
That allocation looks absurd until you've sat in the meeting where it wasn't done.

## The artifacts

- The completed **DACI template**, stating the decision question and the named roles.
- An **options analysis** — the alternatives, with pros, cons, and resource estimates.
- **Action items with owners.**
- **A written record of the final decision and its rationale**, for people who weren't there and for
  the version of your team that will question it in eight months.

That last artifact is the real output. **A decision nobody wrote down will be re-litigated**, and
the re-litigation costs more than the original decision did.

## When Atlassian recommends it

Complex multi-stakeholder decisions, high-stakes strategic choices, cross-functional initiatives,
resource-allocation disputes, and process changes. **Not** for everyday reversible calls — the
overhead isn't worth it, and applying it universally trains people to route around it.

---

## What to steal

1. **Name the Approver before the discussion, not after.** One person. Say it out loud. This single
   habit resolves most stalled decisions.
2. **Separate Driver from Approver.** A junior person can absolutely drive a decision an executive
   approves — and that's the best way to develop them.
3. **"Input, not vote"** is a genuinely useful phrase. Say it explicitly so contributors know whether
   they're being consulted or asked to agree.
4. **Write down the decision *and the reasoning*, and send it to the Informed list.** The reasoning
   is what stops the decision being reopened.
5. **Reserve it for decisions that deserve it.** Reversible, low-stakes calls should just be made.

*(Compare Amazon's single-threaded leader and Basecamp's small senior betting table — three
different companies arriving at the same conclusion: **exactly one person decides, and everyone
knows who it is before the conversation starts.**)*
